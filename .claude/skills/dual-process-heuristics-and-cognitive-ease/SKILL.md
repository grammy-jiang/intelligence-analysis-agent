# Dual Process Heuristics And Cognitive Ease

## Purpose

Audit whether a fast, confident judgment in an analytic product was actually checked by
deliberate reasoning, or whether it rests on an unexamined System-1 shortcut. Under WYSIATI the
mind treats whatever evidence is already at hand as the whole story, quietly substitutes an
easier question for a harder one, and misreads fluency, familiarity, and a coherent-feeling story
for truth — so a hard question can receive a quick, confident answer that was never actually
interrogated by slower reasoning. This skill audits that reasoning process itself — whether a
System-2 check ran, and what shortcut filled the gap when it did not — not the substantive
conclusion the judgment reached.

## When to use

- A hard, effortful question receives a quick, confident answer, with no visible sign a slower,
  second check was ever run.
- The judgment reads as resting on whatever evidence happens to be in hand, with no check on that
  evidence's reliability or on what is missing from it.
- A causal, skill-based, or "recipe for success" account explains a streaky, extreme, or
  lucky-looking result — a hot-hand run, a firm's rise or fall, a leader's reputation, or any past
  success or failure credited to talent or intention rather than chance.
- The write-up credits a conclusion to how familiar, fluent, or instantly coherent it reads, or to
  a fraction-of-a-second sense that it "fits," rather than to independent verification.
- A probability or risk estimate tracks how easily supporting examples come to mind rather than
  their actual frequency.
- The judgment was reached in a notable mood, under heavy competing cognitive load, or amid an
  unnoticed situational cue, and the write-up does not account for it.
- An early, blurry first read of the evidence still appears to be anchoring the current conclusion
  even though clearer information has since arrived.
- Someone's competence, trustworthiness, or authority is being judged mainly from appearance or
  demeanor rather than from a track record.
- The write-up leans on a claimed expert "feel" or pattern recognition, and whether it was earned
  through regular, high-feedback practice is unclear.
- One coherent storyline has absorbed every discrepant detail, with no separate note of what does
  not fit, or an explanation offered that would equally account for two opposite outcomes.

## Procedure

1. Identify the specific judgment under review and the exact question it was meant to answer.
   Check whether that question was actually answered, or whether a quick urge to answer an
   easier, related question was acted on instead; reread the original question before judging the
   answer (P119).
2. Confirm a genuine second, slower pass ran on that judgment rather than accepting the first
   read. An engaged reviewer checks an intuitive answer that came quickly; a "low checker" who
   runs with the first idea is impulsive and error-prone, and System 1 is not readily educable, so
   this check has to be structural, not assumed (P072, P178, P006).
3. Test the judgment against its evidence base: does it treat only the evidence already in hand as
   the whole story, without weighing that evidence's reliability or registering what is missing?
   Worthless or untrustworthy evidence must be discounted to the same status as no evidence at all
   (P022).
4. If the conclusion rests on a causal, skill-based, or "recipe for success" account of an
   extreme, streaky, or lucky-looking result, check whether the underlying group or sample is
   simply small, whether the opposite tail was tested, and whether the account would have made the
   outcome predictable in advance rather than only explicable after the fact (P056, P057, P066).
5. Separate warranted intuition from unwarranted confidence: check whether a claimed "feel" or
   pattern recognition was built in a domain with regular practice and clear feedback, since that
   is what licenses trusting a fast read; that most cognition is unconscious is not, by itself,
   evidence the fast read is accurate (P065, P182).
6. Discount familiarity, fluency, and a smoothly coherent story as evidence of truth. A claim that
   merely feels recognizable, well-fitting, or free of surprise — assessed in a fraction of a
   second — has not thereby been verified; ask what independent check was actually run (P144,
   P179, P186).
7. When a probability or risk estimate is offered, check whether it tracks how easily supporting
   instances came to mind — through fame, salience, or ease of imagining a scenario — rather than
   their true, checkable frequency (P018).
8. Note any situational factor that could have shaped the judgment outside the reasoner's own
   awareness: a notable mood (which raises either credulity or vigilance), heavy competing
   cognitive demand, or an unrelated environmental cue; flag when the write-up does not account
   for it (P140, P071, P064).
9. Check whether an early, blurred, or ambiguous first read of the evidence is still shaping the
   current conclusion. The earlier and longer that impression was held, the clearer the later
   evidence needs to be before the conclusion should be considered updated (P183).
10. If a judgment about a person's competence, trustworthiness, or authority rests mainly on
    appearance or demeanor, flag it regardless of how widely the impression is shared — such
    impressions show high agreement but low accuracy (P146).
11. Check whether a single interpretation was adopted without separately registering the
    discrepant details that do not fit it, or whether the explanation offered would equally
    account for two opposite outcomes; flag both as unearned coherence rather than confirmed
    understanding (P019, P145).
12. Do not let the analyst's seniority, stated incentive to be accurate, or self-reported internal
    consistency stand in for the checks above — heuristic biases are cognitive rather than
    motivational, persist in trained experts, and cannot be detected from the inside (P052).
13. Emit findings highest-impact first, each naming the mechanism, applying its principle ID,
    giving the corrective technique, and stating the residual uncertainty, per the output contract
    below.

## Inputs

- The specific judgment, estimate, or conclusion under review, and the exact question it was
  meant to answer.
- The evidence the analyst had in hand when the judgment was formed, including any known gaps or
  reliability concerns.
- Any causal, skill-based, or "recipe for success" account offered for an extreme, streaky, or
  lucky-looking result.
- Context on how the judgment was reached — how quickly, in what mood or under what workload, and
  whether a second, slower pass was run.
- The basis of any claimed expert "feel" or pattern recognition, including whether it was built
  through regular, high-feedback practice.
- The sequence in which information arrived, when an early or ambiguous first read may still be
  shaping the current view.
- Any appearance- or demeanor-based impression feeding a judgment about a person.

## Output

Per finding: name the bias or perceptual mechanism (attribute substitution, cognitive ease misread
as truth, an illusory pattern in chance, an appearance-driven trait judgment, and so on), apply
the principle(s) by ID, give the corrective technique (reread the actual question, discount
fluency and coherence as evidence, check the sample size and the opposite tail, weigh the track
record behind a claimed intuition), state the residual uncertainty the correction leaves, and end
with a concrete next step. Order findings highest-impact first. This skill critiques how the
judgment was reached; it never supplies the substantive conclusion in its place.

## Anti-patterns to flag

- A hard question answered instantly and confidently, with the easier substituted question never
  surfaced or reread (P119).
- Skipping the second, slower check because the first answer felt obviously right — the mark of a
  low-checking, error-prone process rather than a careful one (P072, P178, P006).
- A conclusion defended as sound because "all the evidence points that way," with no check on that
  evidence's reliability or on what is missing (P022).
- An extreme or streaky result explained by skill, talent, or a "recipe for success," with no
  check on sample size, the opposite tail, or whether the story would have predicted the outcome
  in advance (P056, P057, P066).
- A claim accepted because it reads well, feels familiar, or resolves instantly as coherent, with
  fluency or a fraction-of-a-second sense of fit standing in for verification (P144, P179, P186).
- A probability or risk estimate that tracks how vividly or easily examples come to mind rather
  than their actual frequency (P018).
- A judgment's mood, workload, or incidental surrounding context never screened for, despite
  plausibly shaping how confidently or credulously it was reached (P064, P071, P140).
- An early, blurry impression still driving the conclusion, with no acknowledgment that later,
  clearer evidence should have required more before being accepted (P183).
- A person's competence or trustworthiness inferred mainly from appearance or demeanor, with wide
  agreement on the impression mistaken for its accuracy (P146).
- A "gut feel" or pattern-recognition claim accepted as expertise with no check on whether it was
  earned in a regular, high-feedback domain (P065, P182).
- A single tidy narrative that accounts for all the evidence with no separate note of the details
  that do not fit, or an explanation that would equally justify two contradictory outcomes (P019,
  P145).
- The bias waved off because the analyst is experienced, incentivized to be accurate, or feels
  internally consistent — none of which defends against a heuristic bias from the inside (P052).
