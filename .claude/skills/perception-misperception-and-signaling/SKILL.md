# Perception, Misperception, and Signaling

## Purpose

Review whether an analytic judgment has examined — rather than simply assumed — how an
adversary perceives, misreads, or infers resolve and intent from another actor's signals,
postures, and behavior, and whether the framework used to explain that perception (situational
logic versus bureaucratic-politics theory) was held to its evidentiary requirements rather than
applied as a ready-made template. This skill audits the perception that runs between actors —
how one side reads another's images, signals, and firmness — not the analyst's own mental
models and schemata, which the sibling `mindsets-schemata-and-perception` skill covers.

## When to use

- An assessment rejects an adversary's proposal as unacceptable, or defaults to "splitting the
  difference," without stating what underlying goal or interest the proposal actually serves.
- A judgment argues the adversary's disposition incident by incident — was this move hostile,
  was that one not — rather than setting out and testing two whole, opposed images of that
  adversary.
- A near-term call or a longer-range forecast about the adversary rests entirely on a read of
  its internal mental or bureaucratic workings, with no comparative theory brought in for the
  longer view.
- An adversary's stance, or a friendly government's, is explained as bureaucratic bargaining —
  "where they stand depends on where they sit" — or an outcome is simply labeled a "compromise."
- A judgment applies situational logic, bureaucratic-politics theory, or another general
  framework to an ambiguous case as though the theory settled the matter on its own.
- An assessment recommends or defends a posture or approach toward the adversary without
  stating what inference the adversary is expected to draw from it, or what happens if that
  inference is wrong.
- A judgment claims that firmness or a costly stand on one issue will be read by the adversary
  as narrowly bounded rather than as a sign of wider ambition.
- A recommended conciliatory or de-escalatory initiative toward a possibly aggressive adversary
  is being evaluated with less scrutiny than a comparably deterrent move would receive.

## Procedure

1. Establish the object under review: the adversary's proposal, stance, posture, or signal; the
   account of its meaning that the analytic product gives; and any explanatory framework it
   invokes (situational logic, bureaucratic politics, a theory of resolve or signaling). Fix
   this before judging whether the reading is grounded or merely asserted.
2. Where the product rejects a proposal as unacceptable or defaults to splitting the difference,
   check that it first read the actual goal or interest behind the proposal — not merely its
   surface terms — and examined why it holds its own subgoals; an unexamined means-ends chain
   blocks the search for an integrative option that upgrades both sides' interests (P021).
3. Where the product debates the adversary's disposition incident by incident, check that it
   instead set out two complete, opposed images of the adversary and debated them as wholes;
   the beliefs most needing scrutiny sit at the top of the means-ends chain, and wrong questions
   — not wrong answers — cause the more serious failures (P023).
4. Where a near-term or long-range forecast about the adversary rests on a read of its internal
   mental or bureaucratic process, confirm situational logic is reserved for the short term and
   checked for mirror-imaging, while theory drawn from comparable cases carries the
   longer-range or senior-consumer judgment, since a proximate cause read off the adversary's
   process may be only a symptom (P029).
5. Where the product explains an adversary's stance or signal as bureaucratic bargaining —
   "where they stand depends on where they sit" — confirm the bureaucratic positions were
   specified in advance and shown to explain the outcome, rather than asserted after the fact,
   and that the account does not stop at the bureaucratic level when the distribution of power
   was itself set by an earlier decision of top leadership or the public (P036). Where a
   "compromise" label is offered as the explanation, confirm a theory specified in advance how
   that array of positions would map to the outcome — a bare compromise label fits almost any
   result — and consider whether the clash is better read as one among values held within the
   leadership or society at large than as bureaucratic maneuvering (P037).
6. Where an ambiguous case is settled by applying situational logic, bureaucratic-politics
   theory, or another general framework as a ready-made template, check that the product flags
   the ambiguity in bridging that theory to the specific facts rather than treating the fit as
   automatic, since guidance is scarcest exactly where ambiguity is greatest (P056).
7. Where the product asserts a posture or approach toward the adversary without stating what
   the adversary is expected to infer from it, check for a robust framing — favorable if the
   analyst's assumption about the adversary holds, tolerable if it does not — rather than a
   claim that one posture dominates every reading, and confirm the product does not treat the
   adversary's resulting inference as safely predictable (P069).
8. Where the product claims a firm or costly stand on one issue will be read by the adversary as
   narrowly bounded rather than as a sign of wider ambition, check whether that inference was
   tested — what general principle the firmness is tied to that would let the adversary read it
   as limited — rather than simply asserted (P077).
9. Where the product recommends or evaluates a conciliatory or de-escalatory initiative toward a
   possibly aggressive adversary, confirm it was held to the same scrutiny a deterrent move
   would face: does the analysis examine whether the adversary, in light of what it believes the
   analyst's side knows of its intentions, might read the initiative as evidence the field is
   open to expand rather than as reassurance (P078).
10. Emit findings highest-impact first, each in the name-the-flaw / correction /
    residual-uncertainty / next-step format, and never substitute the reviewer's own reading of
    the adversary for the reading under review.

## Inputs

- The adversary's proposal, stance, posture, or signal under review, and the analytic product's
  account of what it means.
- Any explanatory framework the product invokes — situational logic, bureaucratic-politics
  theory, a theory of resolve or signaling — and the specific bureaucratic positions or
  leadership values it names, if any.
- The image(s) of the adversary in play: the product's own account and, if named, a serious
  rival image.
- The forecast horizon (near-term versus long-range) and the intended audience, so situational
  logic versus theory can be weighed correctly.
- Any stated assumption about what the adversary is expected to infer from a posture, stand, or
  initiative, and the cost if that assumption is wrong.
- Precedent the product cites, or should have cited, for how firmness or a conciliatory move has
  been read by an adversary before.

## Output

Per finding: name the perception or signaling flaw (an unexamined reading of the adversary's
actual goal, an incident-by-incident debate standing in for two whole opposed images, a
mirror-imaged or under-theorized account of the adversary's process, a bureaucratic-politics or
"compromise" label asserted without a predictive theory, a general framework applied to an
ambiguous case as automatic, an untested assumption about what the adversary will infer from a
posture or stand, or a de-escalatory initiative judged more leniently than a deterrent one),
apply the correction (the specific check from the Procedure that exposes it), state the residual
uncertainty the correction leaves about how the adversary actually reads the signal, and end
with a concrete next step. Order findings highest-impact first. Never close a review with the
reviewer's own claim about what the adversary perceives in place of this structure.

## Anti-patterns to flag

- A rejection of an adversary's proposal, or a default to splitting the difference, with no
  reading of the goal or interest actually behind it and no examination of the analyst's own
  means-ends chain (P021).
- A running incident-by-incident debate over whether the adversary is hostile or cooperative
  that never sets out and compares two whole, opposed images of that adversary (P023).
- A near-term or long-range call about the adversary built entirely on a read of its internal
  mental or bureaucratic workings, with no check for mirror-imaging and no comparative theory
  brought in for the longer view (P029).
- An adversary's stance explained as "where they stand depends on where they sit" with the
  bureaucratic positions asserted only after the fact (P036), or a "compromise" label offered
  with no theory that predicted in advance how those positions would map to the outcome, and no
  weighing of a leadership- or society-wide value clash as the deeper cause (P037).
- A general framework — situational logic, bureaucratic politics, or a theory of signaling —
  applied to an ambiguous case as an automatic fit, with the difficulty of bridging that theory
  to the specific facts left unstated (P056).
- A posture or approach toward the adversary recommended as though it dominates every possible
  reading, with no statement of what the adversary is expected to infer or of the cost if that
  inference is wrong (P069).
- A claim that firmness on one issue will read to the adversary as narrowly bounded, asserted
  with no test of what general principle would tie the firmness to a bounded reading (P077).
- A conciliatory or de-escalatory initiative toward a possibly aggressive adversary waved
  through with less scrutiny than a deterrent move would get, never asking whether the adversary
  might read it as an opening to expand rather than as reassurance (P078).
