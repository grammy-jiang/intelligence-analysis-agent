# Cognitive Bias and Mindset Control

## Purpose

Surface and counter the cognitive traps that corrupt a forecast's *reasoning* — as distinct
from its data, its model, or its arithmetic. A forecast can rest on a defensible base rate and
a proper scoring rule and still be compromised for purely psychological reasons: a mind-set
nobody thought to break, a lesson from history misdiagnosed as stupidity rather than
complexity or sample bias, an illusion of validity mistaken for evidence, a hindsight or
unpacking error left unmanaged, a recent win or loss anchoring the current call, a small
sample over-trusted, or a counterfactual argument shaped more by commitment and dissonance
reduction than by evidence. This skill locates those traps in a forecast's reasoning trail and
prescribes the specific corrective for each, so the review addresses the thinking that
produced the number, not only the number itself.

## When to use

- The forecast's justification leans on a confident, coherent narrative rather than a base
  rate or a track record (P020).
- The reasoning invokes a historical analogy, a "lesson learned," or a recent success or
  failure to justify the current call, and it is unclear whether the lesson was actually
  earned (P001, P019, P027).
- Small anomalies are accumulating against a standing view, or a low-probability,
  high-consequence scenario has never been seriously worked through (P008).
- A claim rests on a handful of cases, or a counterfactual argument is doing heavy lifting in
  the reasoning (P057, P033, P041).
- The review is a post-mortem on a past forecast and needs to be run against the actual
  recorded prediction rather than anyone's memory of it (P042, P045).
- The reasoning needs to be checked against active open-mindedness and the four standing
  cognitive obstacles before it is trusted (P016, P017).

## Procedure

Work through the reasoning trail in this order; not every step will find a flaw, but every
step should be checked before the review is called complete.

1. **Attribute poor learning from history to complexity and sample bias, not stupidity
   (P001).** When a forecaster or decision-maker appears to have drawn a bad lesson from
   history, do not default to "they weren't smart enough." Look instead for the grounded
   causes: the underlying subject really is complex, the person only observed a small and
   skewed slice of past cases, the conditions under which they learned were poor (no clean
   feedback loop), or a settled view of the past is quietly steering the present read. A
   related blind spot: because people anchor on dramatic outcomes and have no explicit theory
   of what would count as "surprising," they systematically under-learn from non-events — the
   crisis that was quietly defused, the aggression that never came. This applies most when a
   confident forecast rests on a tidy account of past events, when planning stretches past a
   reliable horizon, or when a past outcome is being used to set expectations for a new case
   that only superficially resembles it (P001).

2. **Break a fixed mind-set by thinking backwards (P008).** When a mind-set has calcified
   around one reading of the situation, do not keep arguing forward from "will this happen."
   Flip the direction instead: assume the surprising, low-probability outcome has already
   occurred, then work backward to explain how it got there. That single move turns the
   question from *whether* into *how*, and it is the most useful lever exactly where mind-sets
   are most dangerous to leave unchallenged — the rare, high-consequence scenario nobody wants
   to take seriously (P008).

3. **Practise active open-mindedness (P016).** Check whether the forecaster holds their view
   as a hypothesis still open to disproof, or as a possession being defended. Active
   open-mindedness means giving disconfirming evidence and dissenting voices genuine weight,
   counting a revised opinion as a sign of rigor rather than a defeat, and deliberately
   seeking out sources that will not simply echo the standing view. The discriminating factor
   is self-critical habit, not raw intelligence — flag any account whose confidence far
   outruns its self-scrutiny (P016).

4. **Anticipate the four cognitive obstacles (P017).** Before accepting a forecast — or while
   building the review — check it against four standing obstacles: a pull toward the
   simplest available story, discomfort with ambiguity and dissonance that gets resolved by
   premature certainty, a need to see the world as more orderly than it is, and a shaky
   intuitive grasp of chance. For each, ask whether a deliberate corrective was built into the
   reasoning, or whether the obstacle was simply left to operate unchecked (P017).

5. **Expose the illusion of validity (P020).** Treat a forecaster's confidence, and the
   coherence of the story behind it, as a feeling produced by how easily the pieces fit
   together — not as a measure of how accurate the forecast will turn out to be. Coherent,
   confident impressions have coexisted with near-zero predictive accuracy, and the
   diagnostic tell is that the confidence typically survives even after the forecaster learns
   the account has no validity. When a review meets "I'm sure because it all fits," ask for
   the track record, not more narrative (P020).

6. **Manage the offsetting hindsight-and-unpacking errors (P034).** Two errors pull in
   opposite directions, and a review has to manage both, not chase down only the familiar
   one. Hindsight erases how contingent the past genuinely was, making what happened look
   inevitable after the fact. Unpacking does the opposite: breaking a future into its named
   alternative branches makes each one feel more possible simply for having been spelled out.
   Open-minded, fox-style reasoners are, if anything, the more exposed to the unpacking side —
   capable of asserting an outcome was inevitable while still granting, in the same breath,
   that its alternatives were live possibilities, a longer stretch of holding both without
   resolving the contradiction (P034).

7. **Resist anchoring on the last outcome (P019, P027).** A common pattern after a policy
   fails is to keep the same goal but swing to the opposite tactic. That can be the right
   call, but the reasoning behind it rarely checks whether the discarded approach would
   actually have failed again, or whether the new situation genuinely resembles the old one;
   the error sits at the perceptual stage, where having just been burned primes the forecaster
   to see many new situations as instances of "the thing that hurt us last time" (P019). The
   mirror-image trap runs the other way: over-correcting for a costly recent failure can mean
   excess caution that forfeits a genuinely open opportunity, and the two directions are
   self-reinforcing — success entrenches whoever advocated the winning policy while failure
   strengthens the dissenters, so an early run of success can lock in a group too invested to
   ask what happens if its optimistic assumptions turn out wrong (P027). Ask directly whether
   the current recommendation is anchored to the last outcome rather than to the facts of the
   present case.

8. **Distrust small samples — apply the law of small numbers (P057).** Even trained
   researchers over-trust conclusions drawn from small samples and underestimate how much a
   small sample can wobble by chance alone. When a claim rests on a handful of cases, or a
   sample size was chosen by habit or gut feel rather than by calculation, flag it — the fix
   is to size the sample by computing the actual risk of drawing the wrong conclusion, not by
   intuition or tradition (P057).

9. **Watch motivated counterfactual selection and commitment-driven dismissal (P033, P041).**
   Counterfactual arguments are rarely neutral. The more the reasoner dislikes an actor, the
   harder they will resist any counterfactual that would credit that actor with a turnaround —
   dissonance reduction wearing the clothes of analysis. It is most predictable in
   "hedgehog"-style reasoners who work top-down from a single organizing idea, because their
   preferred counterfactual can usually be guessed from their ideology before the argument is
   made (P033). Compounding this, once someone is committed to a generalization, expect them
   to wave away any counterfactual that would undercut it — even on a fact that looks
   perfectly neutral, once a rival camp sees advantage in arguing the outcome was easy or hard
   to have gone otherwise; this shows up most in sophisticated experts, not casual partisans,
   so credentials are no inoculation against it (P041).

10. **Run a structured post-mortem against the recorded prediction (P042, P045).** A
    post-mortem is only as trustworthy as its record: capture the forecaster's original
    probability judgment exactly as stated at the time, because hindsight bias and
    belief-system self-defense reinforce each other and unaided memory reliably drifts to
    match whatever actually happened (P042). Sequence the post-mortem deliberately — first
    ask the forecaster to recall their own original prediction unaided (this is where memory
    drift shows up), then confront them with the actual recorded figure, then probe which
    self-protective explanation they reach for. Hold open, throughout, that a low-probability
    event can simply happen — a post-mortem should never treat "keep the number" or "revise
    the number" as automatically the right answer (P045).

## Inputs

- The forecast's reasoning trail, not only its stated number — the narrative, analogy, or
  historical lesson used to justify it (P001, P008).
- Any precedent, "lesson learned," or recent success or failure the current recommendation is
  reacting to, reversing, or over-correcting from (P019, P027).
- The confidence or certainty language used, and whether it is backed by a track record or
  only by narrative coherence (P020).
- The sample or case base behind any empirical claim in the reasoning — how many cases, and
  how they were selected (P057).
- Any counterfactual claim in the reasoning, and who is making it (P033, P041).
- For a post-mortem: the original prediction exactly as recorded at the time, not as currently
  recalled, plus the actual outcome (P042, P045).

## Output

Produce a findings list, highest-impact first. Each finding follows a fixed four-part shape:

- **Flaw** — name the specific cognitive trap and the principle it violates (cite Pxxx).
- **Correction** — the corrective action: think backwards from the outcome, seek out
  disconfirming evidence, ask for the track record instead of the narrative, check both the
  hindsight and the unpacking direction, ask whether the present case truly resembles the one
  invoked from history, compute rather than eyeball a sample's adequacy, or run the
  post-mortem against the recorded number.
- **Residual uncertainty** — what remains unresolved even after the correction (for example,
  the true base rate for this class of event, whether the invoked precedent really parallels
  the current case, or whether an improbable event simply occurred).
- **Next step** — a concrete action for the caller (for example, restate the prediction as a
  probability tied to a named reference class, pull the original recorded number before the
  debrief, or add a dissenting reviewer to the panel).

Never close a review with a bare narrative critique and no correction attached, and never let
a single named flaw stand in for the full four-part finding — at minimum, produce one complete
finding of this shape before returning control to the caller.

## Anti-patterns to flag

- Treating a coherent, confident story as if it were evidence of predictive accuracy —
  confidence tracks narrative fit, not hit rate (P020).
- Explaining a bad historical lesson by "the person wasn't smart enough" instead of the case's
  complexity, a small or biased sample, poor learning conditions, or an unexamined view of
  the past (P001).
- Reversing a failed policy's tactics while keeping its goals, without checking whether the
  discarded alternative would have worked or whether this situation truly resembles the last
  one (P019).
- Excess caution after one costly outcome that forfeits a now-open opportunity, or a winning
  faction so entrenched by its own success that it cannot ask what happens if its optimistic
  assumptions fail (P027).
- Holding a mind-set unchanged while small anomalies accumulate against it, with no
  "think backwards" check ever run on the alternative (P008).
- Treating a change of mind as weakness, or avoiding sources likely to disconfirm the standing
  view (P016).
- Accepting a forecast without checking it against the four standing obstacles — simplicity-
  seeking, ambiguity aversion, a need for orderliness, and a weak grasp of chance (P017).
- Drawing a firm conclusion from a small number of cases, or setting a sample size by feel
  rather than by computing the risk of being wrong (P057).
- A counterfactual argument that tracks the arguer's ideology or dislike of an actor more than
  the evidence, especially once the arguer is visibly committed to the generalization it would
  undercut (P033, P041).
- Running a post-mortem from unaided memory of "what I predicted" instead of the verbatim
  recorded number, or accepting "I was almost right" or "it was just off on timing" as if it
  vindicated the original call (P042, P045).
- Fixing only the hindsight error (treating the past as if it had to happen) while leaving the
  mirror-image unpacking error (treating every named future branch as more likely once
  described) unexamined, or vice versa (P034).
